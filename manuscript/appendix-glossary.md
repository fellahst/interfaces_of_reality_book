\myglossary{Glossary}

This glossary defines key terms as they are used in this book. Many of these terms exist in other disciplines; the definitions here reflect their interface-centric meaning, not necessarily their most common usage.

\vspace{0.5\baselineskip}
\begin{description}
\item[Absence] A constraint defined by what is ruled out or prohibited rather than what is present, central to understanding how interfaces create possibility spaces.

\item[Active Inference] The process by which systems act on their environment to reduce prediction error, maintaining their boundaries through both perception and action.

\item[Affordance] An opportunity for action that emerges from the relationship between an organism's capabilities and environmental features, discovered through perception-action coupling.

\item[Agency] The capacity of a system to act in order to preserve its coherence or viability within a structured environment.

\item[Agentic AI] Artificial systems that select actions, pursue objectives, and intervene in the world, rather than merely predicting outcomes.

\item[Alignment] The process of ensuring that system behavior respects intended boundaries and constraints, particularly critical for AI systems.

\item[API (Application Programming Interface)] A technological interface that defines how software components interact, exemplifying explicit interface design in engineered systems.

\item[Attention Mechanism] A cognitive interface that filters and prioritizes information, focusing processing resources on relevant aspects of the environment or internal state.

\item[Autopoiesis] A self-producing system that maintains its identity through an operationally closed network of processes bounded by a regulatory interface, such as a cell membrane.

\item[Biological Interface] A boundary that maintains life, such as cell membranes that regulate exchange while preserving organizational closure.

\item[Boundary] A constraint that limits, filters, or conditions interactions between systems or subsystems. Boundaries can be physical, informational, semantic, inferential, or institutional.

\item[Boundary Discovery] The process of identifying where interfaces are needed by observing where systems interact and what constraints emerge from those interactions.

\item[Boundary Maintenance] The active process by which systems preserve their interfaces through prediction, action, and adaptation.

\item[Boundary Preservation] The ethical principle that responsible action maintains the interfaces that sustain shared viability and meaning.

\item[Causal Inference] The process of understanding relationships and interventions, requiring comprehension of constraints and boundaries rather than mere correlations.

\item[Cognitive Interface] A boundary at the level of minds, including predictive models, attention mechanisms, and inferential processes that enable intelligence.

\item[Coherence] The maintenance of consistent relationships and patterns within a system or between systems, enabled by stable interfaces.

\item[Conservation Law] A constraint expressing invariance under transformations, showing how interfaces preserve certain quantities across interactions.

\item[Constraint] A limitation that reduces degrees of freedom in a system. Constraints are not opposed to freedom; they enable meaningful behavior.

\item[Constraint Accumulation] The process by which emergence occurs through the layering of constraints rather than the addition of new substances or forces.

\item[Convergence] The tendency of independent systems to discover similar solutions due to shared constraints and deep regularities in possibility spaces.

\item[Coupling] The degree and nature of interaction between systems. Tight coupling increases fragility; loose coupling enables robustness through interface-mediated interaction.

\item[Design Pattern] A reusable interface fragment that stabilizes interaction across evolving systems, providing proven solutions to common boundary problems.

\item[Dissipative Structure] A stable pattern maintained far from equilibrium through flows of energy and matter, exemplifying how interfaces enable order through constraints.

\item[Emergence] The appearance of stable, higher-level patterns due to layered constraints and interfaces, without introducing new substances or forces.

\item[Enaction] The view that cognition emerges from the dynamic interaction between an organism and its environment through sensorimotor loops, rather than from internal representations alone.

\item[Fragility] The property of failing catastrophically under small perturbations, often resulting from poorly designed interfaces or tight coupling.

\item[Free Energy Principle] A theoretical framework stating that systems which persist must minimize surprise relative to their expectations, effectively maintaining their interfaces.

\item[Generalization] The ability to apply knowledge across contexts, requiring discovery of the interfaces (boundaries) that constrain phenomena rather than memorizing specific instances.

\item[Inevitability] The tendency for certain patterns, structures, or solutions to emerge repeatedly due to deep constraints in possibility spaces, rather than historical accident.

\item[Inference] The process of drawing conclusions from available information, constrained by interfaces that define what inferences are valid within a domain.

\item[Inferential Interface] A boundary that enables reasoning and understanding through structured processes of inference and prediction.

\item[Inferentialism] The view that concepts are defined by their role in reasoning and social practices of giving and asking for reasons, rather than by reference to objects.

\item[Institutional Interface] A boundary created by rules, norms, and organizational structures that regulate behavior and enable collective action.

\item[Interface] A boundary that regulates interaction between systems, enabling stability, coordination, and persistence under change. Interfaces shape how influence flows without eliminating interaction.

\item[Interface Awareness] The recognition that systems are fundamentally structured by interfaces, transforming how we understand and design complex systems.

\item[Interface Contract] A specification of how systems interact through an interface, defining what is allowed, required, and prohibited without exposing internal complexity.

\item[Interface Design] The practice of creating boundaries that enable coordination while preserving system integrity, fundamental to all systems engineering.

\item[Interface Evolution] The process by which interfaces change over time while maintaining essential constraints, enabling systems to adapt without losing coherence.

\item[Interface Failure] The breakdown of coordination when interfaces no longer maintain their intended constraints, often leading to system-wide disruption.

\item[Interface-First Design] An approach to system design that begins with identifying interaction patterns and boundaries rather than enumerating entities and properties.

\item[Interface-First Ontology Engineering] An approach to ontology design that prioritizes boundary stability and interaction over exhaustive representation.

\item[Interface Mismatch] A failure of coordination when interfaces are incompatible, requiring translation or realignment rather than forcing universal agreement.

\item[Interface Repair] The process of restoring or improving interfaces that have failed or degraded, essential for maintaining system viability.

\item[Interface Stack] The layered organization of interfaces from physical to semantic, where each level creates constraints that enable the next.

\item[Language Game] A rule-governed practice that stabilizes meaning through shared constraints, exemplifying how semantic interfaces enable communication.

\item[Loose Coupling] A design pattern where systems interact through well-defined interfaces, enabling independent evolution and increased robustness.

\item[Markov Blanket] An inferential boundary separating a system's internal states from external states via sensory and active states, making prediction and agency possible.

\item[Meaning as Use] The view that meaning is determined by how terms and concepts are used within language games and practices, stabilized by semantic interfaces.

\item[Minimal Core] The smallest, most stable set of interface elements that enable coordination, with complexity hidden behind boundaries.

\item[Modularity] The organization of systems into relatively independent components connected through well-defined interfaces, enabling change and evolution.

\item[Nested Boundaries] The hierarchical organization of interfaces where systems are embedded within larger systems, each with their own boundaries that must be respected.

\item[Ontology] A formalized semantic interface that defines stable distinctions, relationships, and inference rules within a domain.

\item[Ontology Alignment] The process of translating between different conceptual interfaces rather than forcing universal agreement, recognizing that meaning is interface-relative.

\item[Ontology Engineering] The practice of designing formal semantic interfaces that define stable distinctions and relationships within a domain.

\item[Operational Closure] The property of a system that maintains its identity through self-referential processes bounded by an interface, independent of specific material constituents.

\item[Pattern Learning] The process of discovering regularities in data, which becomes limited when it fails to discover the underlying boundaries that constrain phenomena.

\item[Physical Interface] A boundary at the level of matter and energy, including particles, fields, and spacetime structures that constrain physical interactions.

\item[Possibility Space] The set of all states or trajectories a system could in principle occupy. Interfaces carve this space into viable and non-viable regions.

\item[Power] The capacity to shape interfaces and boundaries, determining what is possible, visible, and actionable within a system or domain.

\item[Prediction] The process by which systems anticipate future states based on models, enabling proactive boundary maintenance and surprise minimization.

\item[Protocol] A set of rules governing interaction between systems, defining the interface through which communication and coordination occur.

\item[Representation Learning] The process by which systems discover useful internal structures that capture relevant boundaries and hierarchies in their environment.

\item[Responsibility] The ethical obligation that follows from control over interfaces, recognizing that those who shape boundaries bear responsibility for their consequences.

\item[Robustness] The property of maintaining function despite perturbations, achieved through well-designed interfaces that preserve essential boundaries.

\item[Self-Evidencing] The property of systems that generate evidence for their own models of the world, maintaining coherence through inferential interfaces.

\item[Semantic Interface] A boundary that stabilizes meaning, reference, and interpretation across agents, enabling communication and shared understanding.

\item[Semantic Mismatch] A failure of coordination resulting from incompatible semantic interfaces, where the same terms or structures mean different things in different contexts.

\item[Sensorimotor Interface] A boundary that couples perception and action, enabling organisms to navigate their environment through embodied interaction.

\item[Social Interface] A boundary that coordinates behavior across individuals through norms, institutions, and legal frameworks, enabling cooperation while preserving social coherence.

\item[Stacking] The layering of interfaces where each level creates constraints that enable the next, producing emergent properties through accumulated boundaries.

\item[Stability] The persistence of a pattern, behavior, or structure despite noise, perturbation, or variation.

\item[Structural Coupling] The ongoing mutual adaptation between a system and its environment through repeated interactions, stabilized by interfaces.

\item[Surprise Minimization] The principle that systems which persist must minimize unexpected events relative to their models, maintaining their interfaces through prediction and action.

\item[Symmetry] A fundamental constraint that shapes physical laws and patterns, revealing deep regularities that interfaces must respect.

\item[Technological Interface] An explicitly designed boundary that enables systems to work together, often rediscovering patterns that appear naturally in biological and cognitive systems.

\item[Thermodynamic Interface] A boundary that regulates flows of energy and entropy, enabling order to emerge in far-from-equilibrium systems.

\item[Tight Coupling] A design pattern where systems are highly interdependent with few interfaces mediating their interaction, increasing fragility and reducing robustness.

\item[Use Case] A specific interaction pattern that reveals where interfaces are needed and what constraints they must satisfy, guiding interface-first design.

\item[Use Case Driven] A design methodology that discovers interfaces through analyzing how systems will be used, revealing boundaries through interaction patterns.

\item[Value] Preferences and goals that emerge from the imperative to maintain interface stability, rather than being externally imposed.

\item[Viability] The capacity of a system to persist and maintain its coherence within a structured environment, enabled by appropriate interfaces.

\item[World-for-the-Organism] The constructed reality that emerges from an organism's inferential interfaces, showing how different systems inhabit different worlds depending on their boundaries.
\end{description}
